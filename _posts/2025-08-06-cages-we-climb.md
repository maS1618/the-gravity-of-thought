Why are the cages we climb the most comforting traps of all? 

Is it the illusion of movement with a non-circular end goal? Is the journey (the climb) just an illusion of questions leading to an answer. 

Have you ever come across an answer that was not also a question? 

  


  


Imagine yourself small; A spider’s web* is a trampoline to your own death – 

but even after reading this you are tempted - you cannot erase from your mind the neurochemical memory of those intricate silver threads, the buoyant safety net : 

you are buoyant, 

it is fighting its own nature every time you are able to bounce back up without being entangled to your slow devouring. 

  


Imagine yourself climbing – through a  [_jungle gym_](https://www.therecordnorthshore.org/2023/10/25/a-time-to-climb-origins-of-jungle-gym-began-100-years-ago-in-winnetka/) metal crystal, designed** to let your body explore 3 dimensions, free up your mind to dream of 4. 

  


One rung up - hook your foot against the metal bar – do you feel in control? Have you considered all the parameters of this system, though. Who determines when your muscles start to crumble under the pain, while you are launching yourself through the space between these bars? When you are not letting them control your position in space, who has a say over what your legs are doing? 

  


Let go for a moment of the fear of  “pain” that only arises from the experience of inhabiting a physical body, and explore the internal narrative of an abstractly trapped entity.

  


i.e., how do your thoughts respond to being in a cage? And if you can see the world outside, are you really impeded (that is, does your mind even feel caged if its observation of the world is not)? 

  


My first instinctive reaction to being trapped is to reach out and touch the components that make up a cage – as if my curiosity in their atoms, in the mechanisms of their existence, as if my sincere desire to hold them would instantly dismantle this architecture.

  


As a result, my first learning is that no dismantling is ever instantaneous. But a touch is volatile and can set off a chain reaction. That is to say, you might not be able to dismantle a system from within using the short, linear path you so clearly see in your mind’s eye; but if you are sincere and push in a direction you deem worthy – the system will crumble anyway, more suddenly and harder than you could’ve possibly anticipated. 

  


Without being entranced by it, though, consider where the beauty of these contraptions stems from: 

  


Is it the predictable ( therefore comforting ) periodicity of any concept structurally integrated into the walls of a cage? 

Is it the luxury of being able to look onto the outside world while cocooned in the safety of this one? 

Is that why it takes a threshold amount of insanity before we attempt climbing out in the first place? 

  


That’s half the cases, I believe. 

  


In the other half, it just takes that long to tear our gaze away from the  enchanting world outside and slowly look up. Slowly realize it’s a trap. Slowly stop shaking our fists at the mad people and start shaking hands with them on our way up, high-fiving on our fall down. 

  


Like ants – but less disciplined; 

the embodiment of chaos

in more dimensions than they have the guts to count. 

  


on a mission – but we always stop to hug when we run into each other. 

  


P.S.: The best architectures then have the safety of structure without a trapping of the soul: cages without confinement, if you can build that. 

  


*A spider’s web considered here to be a 2 dimensional cage, if you will. 

  


** Designed by [Charles Howard Hinton](https://www.sciencefocus.com/science/the-four-dimensional-life-of-mathematician-charles-howard-hinton), a mathematician and philosopher, who wanted his kids to be able to “think in the 4 dimensions” when they grew up, which he imagined would be initiated by their profound habituation with the 3 dimensions from an early age.
