So we come down to rocketry. The fact that after playing with pendulums and respectfully defying gravity, we

don't explain ourselves. Not with silly, heart wrenching solutions to inevitable accommodation problems- 

not to mine diamonds on Neptune. We build rockets because we want to go somewhere, or sometimes

just because we want to see something shiny shoot across the sky. And even unintentionally, isn't that 

a better explanation than mining ? 

Our craft is propelled by a solid fuel rather romantically christened Sugarcandy ( 65% KNO3 , 34% sugar, 

1% Sulphurcatalyst for more thrust generation ). 

**  
**

Our solid propellant works in rocket motors, which we can build from scratch (that's another reason you

don't mess with scientists with drive- they know all they're fighting for. What can you destroy?)

  


One problem though. A circle at the bottom of the body tube can't generate enough thrust to lift the whole rocket.

So we drillstrong word  a hole through the middle of the propellant, dramatically increasing the amount of propellant

burning at a given time, along with the thrust*. 

  


*I’m still not over the genius of the idea. 

**  
**

Now since we have a narrow passage to light the propellant all at once, we need an igniter

{which, in the spirit of raw art, we make ourselves}. 

The device is pretty simple, and thus, here, self explanatory. 

  


Through the din of all the engineering problems, though, we must remember what the rocket is meant 

to do- fly. And for that it needs stability. Which comes through a subtle of 2 points - the centres of mass and pressure. 

  


Practically, it can be found by suspending the (model) rocket by a string: the

point of suspension at which it is horizontal is the centre of mass. 

**  
**

Essentially, there are 3 ways we could place these points with respect to one another. 

  


I, for one, only know the experimental proof for this stability. 

01.Imagine holding (or better yet, hold) a pen by its tip, such that it stands erect hinged at  


your fingers. The lower end thus becomes the centre of mass. If pressure (a nudge) is

applied above this point, the rocket is clearly disbalanced, and flips.   
02\. Grab it by its centre, and try hitting that point (the C.O.M. itself). The centres of mass 

and pressure (where your fingers hold the pen) will always stay together, but if an external 

force acts on any other point on the body tube, the rocket goes into a spin.   
03\. So hold the tip of the pen now, such that the rest of the body hangs free below the 

centre of mass. Any force applied now may temporarily disturb the rocket’s position,

but it will eventually fall back into its original state.   





**  
**

This rocket would pierce the air about 600-800 m deep, after which, the obvious question: 

**  
**

Does it really have to come down? Can't we devise some (desperate) mechanism to keep

it floating in a blissful middle ground -forever? 

  


No. Unless we want a blue sky cluttered with unambitious debris that wasn't built to go up but

not exactly to come down either {occasionally failing,consequently falling, and causing accidents

no spineless middle-grounder would take responsibility for}. 

  


So our craft must come down, balanced peacefully on a parachute that brings back a souvenir for when we reach the stars, 

and smile -dazed- at the time we had glanced tentatively at the sky.
